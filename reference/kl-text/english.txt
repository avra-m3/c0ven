The Lighthouse Keeper's Ledger

When the last keeper left the lighthouse on Carrow Point, he took the brass telescope, two blankets and a cat, and he left behind a ledger. It was a thick book bound in green cloth, its spine cracked from forty years of being opened flat on a desk beside the window. Nobody at the harbour office thought to collect it. For almost a decade it sat in a drawer in the keeper's cottage, under a tin of matches and a folded tide table, until a girl named Maren Holt found it on a wet afternoon in March.

Maren was eleven that spring, and she had been sent to the cottage with her uncle, who had bought it cheaply from the council and meant to turn it into a place for walkers to stay. He spent the day pulling up rotten floorboards. She spent it opening every drawer and cupboard she could reach. Most of them held nothing but dust and the dry bodies of moths. The ledger was the first thing she found that seemed to want reading.

The entries began in a careful, upright hand. Each one gave the date, the hour, the direction and strength of the wind, the state of the sea, and the number of ships that had passed within sight of the light. Some days there were none. On others the keeper had counted fourteen or fifteen: colliers heading south with coal, fishing boats returning before a storm, once a grey warship that did not answer his signal. At the bottom of each page he had added a line or two about whatever else had happened. "Gannets diving off the north rocks all morning." "Lamp lit at 4.12, late, the wick needed trimming." "Letter from my sister. She says the apples have done well."

At first Maren read only those last lines, skipping the columns of numbers. But after a while she began to notice that the numbers told a story of their own. In the winters the wind came mostly from the west and the ships were fewer. In the summers there were more, and smaller, and they came closer to the shore. One year, she noticed, the count dropped sharply in October and stayed low until the following spring. The keeper had written nothing about why. She turned the pages back and forth, looking for a clue, and found only a single line in the margin of a November page: "Quiet. They say the yard at Hessel has closed."

Her uncle did not know what the yard at Hessel had been. Neither did the woman who ran the shop in the village, though she remembered her grandfather talking about the ships that used to be built there. It was the librarian in the town, a patient man with a cardigan buttoned to the throat, who finally found the answer in an old newspaper. The shipyard had built small cargo boats for coastal trade, and when it closed, two hundred men lost their work in a single week. The boats it had built kept sailing for years afterwards, but no new ones joined them, and slowly the coast grew quieter.

Maren copied the counts from the ledger into an exercise book, one year to a page, and drew a line through them in pencil. The line rose and fell with the seasons, like breathing, and then it fell and did not come back up. She showed it to her uncle, who looked at it for a long time and then said that he had not known a book of weather could hold so much.

Over the following weeks she read the whole ledger, from the first page to the last. She learned that the keeper had married late, that his wife had come to live at the point for six years and then gone back to the town, and that he had written to her every Sunday afterwards. She learned that he had once rowed out alone in a gale to a fishing boat that had lost its rudder, and that the only thing he wrote about it was the time he got back and the words "all three safe." She learned that he liked bread with caraway seeds in it, that he was afraid of thunder, and that he had kept a list, on the inside of the back cover, of every bird he had ever seen from the gallery around the lamp.

The last entry was short. It gave the date and the wind, and then it said: "Light made automatic today. Engineer very pleasant. I am to go to my sister's. The sea is the same as it was yesterday, which is a comfort."

Maren closed the book and sat for a while with it on her knees. Outside, the light had begun to turn on its own, as it had every evening for ten years, without anyone to trim the wick or write down the hour. Somewhere beyond the rocks a single boat was heading home, its own small lamp swinging at the masthead. She did not know whose it was. She opened her exercise book to a fresh page, wrote the date at the top, and added one ship to the count.

Years later, when the cottage had become the guesthouse her uncle had planned, the ledger stayed on a shelf in the front room, beside a map of the coast and a jar of sea glass. Walkers who stayed there sometimes took it down and leafed through it in the evenings. Most of them read only the lines at the bottom of the pages. A few, the ones who stayed longer, began to count the ships.
